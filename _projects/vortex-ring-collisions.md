A research question I started during my post-doc is the simulation of the [head-on collision of two ring vortices](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XJk8ijAUCiI), to study the turbulent cascade. Two counter-rotating rings colliding head-on disintegrate into smoke in a matter of milliseconds, providing a canonical system for studying the fast formation of minute (turbulent) structures from relatively smooth initial conditions. Experiments find it hard to measure everything that is going on, but simulations have a hard time figuring out which are the correct initial conditions to use: very smooth initial conditions can produce very different physics to conditions with a degree of noise.

High-speed visualization techniques have provided us with a new method to study these collisions. In this [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_z099yZzQik), submitted to the Gallery of Fluid Motion, we can see indications of a mechanism similar to a turbulent cascade appearing, which transfers energy across scales. During several years, we worked to characterize the system both experimentally and numerically, and related it to the interaction between counter-rotating vortex tubes. We found [evidence of iterative instabilities](https://www.science.org/doi/full/10.1126/sciadv.aaz2717), and [related those to the energy transfer in turbulence](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1367-2630/acffeb/meta), finding that vortex tubes and rings were not a good model of the turbulent cascade. During the process, we characterized the development of instabilities in vortex rings when they interact [with each other, or with walls and free-slip surfaces](https://journals.aps.org/prfluids/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevFluids.6.104702). We also studied the [interaction of vortex tubes](https://journals.aps.org/prfluids/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevFluids.6.104702) at different angles, finding regions where vortex reconnection or iterative instabilities dominate.

As a final side-project, we investigated the effect of a magnetic field on vortex-tube interaction, due to the relevance of this process in the formation of sunspots. This led to a [manuscript](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-fluid-mechanics/article/vortexmagnetic-competition-and-regime-transitions-in-antiparallel-flux-tubes/20BEED925424863ABD5223087BC2A018), and a [second award-winning video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NqLNvBXtCQs) where we separated different regimes of magnetically and vorticity dominated interactions, finding that vortices with a strong magnetic field tend to be very stable in situations where they would normally disintegrate.
